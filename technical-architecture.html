Technical Architecture

Turning the design hypothesis into a testable system

The technical architecture translates the CAMPUS conceptual model into a software and infrastructure structure that can be prototyped, tested, secured, integrated, and revised. It must preserve the relationship-first direction while avoiding premature commitment to a database, framework, cloud provider, or production topology.

Technical choices in this chapter are therefore implementation hypotheses. They should be evaluated through prototype experiments, institutional constraints, user needs, operational capability, security requirements, and evidence from validation.

Technical Boundary

The conceptual architecture defines what the system must represent. The technical architecture investigates how those requirements can be implemented responsibly and at a scale appropriate to the R&D stage.

Technical principles

  • 01 Relationship-first representation and traversal.
  • 02 Coexistence with specialized institutional systems.
  • 03 Explicit authority and source-of-truth boundaries.
  • 04 Privacy, access control, and minimization by design.
  • 05 Structured and traceable provenance.
  • 06 Extensible entities, relationships, and interfaces.
  • 07 Deployability with modest infrastructure.
  • 08 Capability for local-first or hybrid operation.
  • 09 Technology neutrality until evidence supports a choice.
  • 10 Evidence-driven evolution.

Logical architecture

The proposed logical structure contains an Experience Layer for web, mobile, spatial, administrative, and community views; an Application Layer for discovery, profiles, relationships, knowledge, places, activities, services, and opportunities; and a Relational Domain Layer for entities, relationships, temporal state, context, provenance, identity references, and visibility.

Below these sit the Discovery Layer for search, indexing, filtering, traversal, and ranking; the Integration Layer for APIs, imports, exports, events, synchronization, identity federation, and external references; the Data Layer for operational and relationship data, metadata, search indexes, audit information, and configuration; and the Infrastructure Layer for networks, servers, cloud, storage, devices, monitoring, backups, and security infrastructure.

Experience and application layers

The initial prototype should prioritize a responsive web interface as a low-friction way to test the relational model across devices. Potential future interfaces include mobile, kiosk, spatial or map, administrative, community, research, and academic views.

Application capabilities should remain coherent rather than becoming independent applications. A profile, for example, should expose related research, organizations, activities, and places through the same relationship model.

Relational domain model

The domain layer should provide a consistent model for entities, relationships, attributes, temporal state, provenance, authority, visibility, permissions, lifecycle, identifiers, and context. A provisional entity contains an identifier, type, label, description, status, metadata, provenance, authority, visibility, timestamps, and relationships.

Relationships should be explicit objects with an identifier, source, type, target, status, validity period, provenance, authority, visibility, and metadata. This distinguishes current membership from former membership or a planned relationship rather than collapsing them into a generic link.

Identifiers and temporal data

Entities should have stable identifiers independent of presentation URLs or interfaces. This supports persistent references, synchronization, integration, continuity, migration, and multiple interfaces. External institutional identifiers may be mapped to CAMPUS identifiers without making CAMPUS authoritative over them.

Temporal state should distinguish current, historical, future, temporary, and expired relationships. The prototype may use a simplified temporal model while preserving the possibility of richer history.

Search and discovery architecture

Search must support entity search, attribute filtering, relationship traversal, contextual discovery, and potentially semantic discovery where justified. A search index may contain denormalized or derived information for retrieval, but it must not automatically become the authoritative source.

Ranking may consider textual relevance, relationship relevance, organization, location, time, authority, freshness, permissions, and context. Ranking should remain understandable and inspectable rather than becoming an opaque mechanism that silently determines what users can discover.

Identity and authorization

The architecture should separate authentication, identity, role or affiliation, authorization, and access to entities and relationships. Institutional identity providers may eventually provide authentication, while CAMPUS maintains application-specific authorization and relationship context.

Authorization may operate at system, organization, entity, relationship, action, field, or attribute level. The target is least-privilege access. A prototype identity mechanism may be used for experimentation when institutional identity integration is unavailable, but it must not be presented as a production university identity system.

Provenance and data authority

Provenance should be structured rather than merely displayed as a citation. Potential fields include source, author, publisher or authority, creation and update dates, verification status, import method, external identifier, and version.

The architecture must distinguish the source of truth from the CAMPUS representation. CAMPUS may store enough information to support discovery without assuming ownership of an authoritative record.

Authority Principle

CAMPUS should connect information without silently becoming the authority over information that belongs to another institutional system.

Integration architecture

Integration should support several patterns: reference to an external resource, approved import, controlled synchronization, dynamic query, event-based updates, and identity or service federation. The correct pattern depends on the system and data involved.

A dedicated integration gateway should separate external systems from the CAMPUS domain. It can contain API adapters, data mapping, validation, authentication, synchronization, and integration policy, reducing direct coupling between every CAMPUS component and every external system.

External systems rarely use identical vocabularies. Mapping an external student record to a CAMPUS Person, an academic unit to an Organization, or a course record to a Course must preserve the source semantics and authority rather than assuming similarly named fields mean the same thing.

Events, synchronization, and local-first operation

A future deployment may process changes from source systems through an event or synchronization layer, validation, domain updates, and search index updates. Multiple independent local nodes make conflict resolution, identity, offline authorization, freshness, encryption, backup, recovery, monitoring, and node registration more complex.

A hybrid deployment could combine external services, an API or sync boundary, a local CAMPUS node, and campus users, data, and services. This is a technical research area, not an established production architecture.

Storage strategy

No database technology is mandated. A relational database offers mature transactions, structured data, integrity constraints, familiar tools, and broad operational support. A graph database may make explicit relationships and traversal natural but can add operational and integration complexity. A hybrid of relational storage, graph-oriented structures, search indexes, and specialized stores may be appropriate.

Document-oriented storage may help with some content types but should not replace the conceptual relationship model merely because content is document-like. The final choice should follow experiments involving relationship queries, transactions, search, integration, scale, local deployment, developer capability, and operational burden.

Prototype technology selection

Prototype technologies should be mature, accessible to the team, inexpensive to operate, documented, portable, testable, suitable for local deployment, and replaceable where practical. A prototype choice is an implementation decision, not automatically a permanent CAMPUS architecture decision.

Security architecture

Security is cross-cutting rather than a final deployment task. It must address identity security, authorization, data protection, provenance integrity, secure integration, input validation, auditability, secrets, backups, monitoring, incident response, and least-privilege operation.

Threat modeling should consider unauthorized access, incorrect or malicious relationships, provenance manipulation, data leakage, integration compromise, synchronization conflicts, account takeover, infrastructure failure, and abuse of discovery or ranking mechanisms.

Technical Status

The architecture is a provisional technical direction derived from the conceptual model and MVP boundary. Database, framework, infrastructure, identity, synchronization, search, GIS, and AI choices remain open until experiments and institutional requirements provide sufficient evidence.
