Major Dimensions

Where the relational architecture may lead

The CAMPUS architecture is intentionally extensible. The relational core can support multiple domains of university activity, referred to here as CAMPUS dimensions. A dimension is a coherent area in which relationships may produce useful capabilities, research questions, or future applications.

The existence of a dimension is not a commitment to implement it. Each must be evaluated against the problem it addresses, users affected, evidence of need, its contribution to the relational model, existing systems, technical requirements, governance, privacy, security, feasibility, MVP relevance, and evidence required before expansion.

Dimension Categories

Foundational capabilities establish and test the relational model. Developmental dimensions build on a validated foundation. Exploratory dimensions are longer-term or research-oriented directions whose value and feasibility still require investigation.

1. Digital Space / Community and Knowledge

Digital Space is the most direct expression of the CAMPUS relational model: a digital environment where users can discover and interact with people, organizations, knowledge, activities, services, places, and opportunities.

It combines community interaction with institutional, scholarly, and community-generated knowledge. Potential capabilities include profiles, organization pages, institutional knowledge, contributions, discussions, events, relationship navigation, contextual search, place discovery, opportunity discovery, service discovery, and activity feeds.

This is the most direct MVP expression of the core architecture. The smallest set of capabilities should be selected to test the relational hypothesis, with provenance, authority, and moderation treated as foundational concerns.

2. Curriculum Continuity and Learning Records

CCLR explores how CAMPUS could represent educational relationships across time: curriculum, courses, learning activities, competencies, projects, instructors, resources, and progression.

It should not assume ownership of official academic records. Student records, grades, enrollment, and other authoritative data may remain in existing systems. CCLR investigates how educational relationships can be represented while respecting those systems and applicable privacy requirements.

CCLR is a later-phase research direction. Selected educational relationships may inform the foundation, but a complete learning record system is outside the initial MVP.

3. Pathways / Education-to-Career Continuity

Pathways explores relationships connecting education with future opportunities and transitions. Relevant connections may include courses, competencies, projects, research, organizations, internships, alumni, employers, and careers.

Potential capabilities include opportunity discovery, competency relationships, alumni connections, internship discovery, research-to-career pathways, project portfolios, and education-to-opportunity navigation. Its value depends on reliable relationships among people, education, activities, research, and opportunities.

Pathways is a later-phase dimension requiring research with students, alumni, career services, employers, and partners, as well as privacy, consent, and opportunity-verification work.

4. Research Discovery and Collaboration

This dimension explores CAMPUS as a discovery layer for relationships among researchers, expertise, projects, publications, laboratories, facilities, students, organizations, datasets, communities, and collaboration opportunities.

Potential capabilities include expertise discovery, researcher profiles, project discovery, publication relationships, laboratory and facility discovery, collaboration discovery, and research opportunities. Repositories and research-management systems may remain authoritative while CAMPUS makes connections among their resources easier to discover.

A small research use case may inform the MVP, but a complete research collaboration environment is a later-phase direction.

5. Spatial Campus / Digital Twin

This dimension represents the physical university as part of the relational environment. It ranges from location-aware discovery to more sophisticated digital-twin representations, which should not be treated as equivalent capabilities.

Location → Spatial relationships → Interactive campus model → Digital twin

Foundational spatial capabilities may include campus maps, facility discovery, room and service relationships, event locations, resource locations, and spatial search. Places are already first-class conceptual entities, so spatial features extend the relational model rather than creating a disconnected mapping application.

Basic place representation may belong in the MVP. Advanced GIS is a later phase, while a full digital twin remains exploratory until it demonstrates actual user or institutional value.

6. Local-First Digital Campus Infrastructure

Local-first is an architectural dimension rather than a user-facing product. It investigates whether selected CAMPUS capabilities can operate reliably within university-controlled infrastructure while maintaining appropriate synchronization with external systems.

Benefits to investigate include resilience, continuity, local responsiveness, reduced dependence on continuous Internet access, institutional control, controlled synchronization, and data stewardship. Costs include infrastructure, maintenance, conflict resolution, security, monitoring, redundancy, hardware, and technical staffing.

A small local-first experiment may be appropriate during prototyping, but a complete local-first deployment is not an MVP commitment.

7. GIS, Analytics, and Future AI

These capabilities may become valuable once the foundational relational model is mature, but they should not drive the architecture prematurely.

GIS could extend spatial discovery, facilities analysis, environmental context, infrastructure planning, and community relationships. Analytics could support relationship analysis, resource utilization, participation patterns, opportunity discovery, spatial analysis, and network analysis, subject to significant governance requirements.

AI could support semantic search, question answering, knowledge discovery, recommendations, summarization, relationship exploration, and research discovery. It is deliberately not the foundation of CAMPUS. Reliable identity, provenance, relationships, permissions, and governance must come first.

Dependency Principle

Later dimensions should consume and extend validated foundational capabilities rather than requiring independent parallel architectures.

Dimension classification

  • 01 Digital Space / Community and Knowledge: MVP and foundational.
  • 02 CCLR: later-phase educational continuity.
  • 03 Pathways: later-phase education-to-career continuity.
  • 04 Research Discovery: later-phase research relationships.
  • 05 Spatial Campus: basic MVP, advanced later.
  • 06 Digital Twin: exploratory.
  • 07 Local-First: architectural research direction.
  • 08 GIS and Analytics: later-phase.
  • 09 AI: future research direction.

Scope discipline

A proposed capability should answer nine questions before entering committed development scope: What problem does it address? Who needs it? What evidence supports the need? What CAMPUS relationship does it depend on? Does it strengthen the relational architecture? Does it require replacing an existing system? What new privacy, security, governance, or infrastructure requirements does it create? Can it be validated independently? Does it belong in the MVP, a later phase, or research only?

If these questions cannot be answered, the capability should remain an open research idea rather than becoming an implicit feature commitment.

Development principle

Foundation → Validation → Selected Dimensions → Advanced Research

CAMPUS should first establish the relational core, identity, knowledge, people, organizations, places, and discovery. After validation, selected dimensions such as CCLR, Pathways, Research, and Spatial capabilities can be evaluated. Digital twins, GIS, analytics, expanded local-first infrastructure, and AI belong to advanced research unless evidence changes that classification.

Major Dimensions Summary

The dimensions describe how CAMPUS may eventually extend across the university, but they do not expand the MVP by default. Their value must be earned through evidence, dependency on the relational core, and bounded validation.
