CAMPUS Conceptual Architecture

The relational structure behind the project

The CAMPUS conceptual architecture defines the relationships among the principal entities, capabilities, boundaries, and future dimensions of the system. It provides the foundation from which the MVP, technical architecture, prototype, research experiments, and future development can be derived.

The architecture is intentionally broader than the initial MVP. It describes the architectural space being investigated, while research and validation determine which parts should actually be implemented.

Core Architectural Principle

Represent the university not only as a collection of information and services, but as a network of meaningful relationships among people, organizations, knowledge, education, research, places, activities, services, opportunities, and communities.

Conceptual layers

CAMPUS can be understood through five broad conceptual layers:

CAMPUS Experience → Relational Campus Layer → Knowledge and Discovery → Integration and Systems → University Infrastructure

The experience layer includes search, discovery, profiles, communities, places, pathways, activities, knowledge, opportunities, and services. The relational layer represents entities, relationships, context, time, provenance, identity, permissions, status, and visibility. The lower layers connect discovery to institutional systems, networks, devices, facilities, and the physical campus.

The relational campus layer

The Relational Campus Layer is the conceptual core of CAMPUS. It represents entities and the relationships among them while preserving the context required to interpret those relationships.

A person may be a member of an organization that hosts an event at a place containing a facility. The important information is not only that these entities exist, but who established the relationship, when it is valid, whether it is active, who can see it, what authority supports it, and what other relationships can be discovered from it.

Core entity domains

The current conceptual model identifies people, organizations, education, knowledge, research, places and facilities, activities, opportunities, and services and resources. These are conceptual domains, not finalized database tables or object types.

Entity Domains

People and roles; academic units and communities; programs, courses, and learning activities; documents and scholarly knowledge; research projects and outputs; campuses, buildings, rooms, and equipment; events and other activities; scholarships, internships, training, and projects; and institutional or community services.

People may have several simultaneous or historical relationships with the university. Organizations may contain, own, operate, host, sponsor, administer, or participate in other entities. Knowledge objects should preserve provenance, authority, status, and visibility. Spatial entities should participate in discovery without requiring a complete digital twin.

Relationships as first-class objects

Relationships should be treated as meaningful objects rather than incidental links. A relationship may contain a source, target, type, status, start, end, authority, provenance, visibility, permissions, and context.

This allows CAMPUS to represent a teaching relationship for a particular term, an authorship relationship with publication metadata, or a facility's location within a building. Relationships can therefore have their own provenance, temporal validity, permissions, and contextual meaning.

Time and continuity

University relationships change. The architecture should distinguish current, historical, planned, temporary, recurring, and expired relationships. This provides a conceptual foundation for continuity without requiring all historical information to remain publicly visible.

Identity, authority, and visibility

A relational architecture requires reliable identification of entities. CAMPUS therefore treats authentication, identity, roles, organizational affiliation, authorization, permissions, account lifecycle, relationship ownership, institutional authority, and potentially federated identities as foundational concerns.

CAMPUS should not automatically create a second identity infrastructure where appropriate institutional services already exist. The exact identity architecture remains unresolved.

Provenance and authority are part of the system's trust model. The architecture must distinguish official institutional information, community contributions, personal opinions, unverified information, and scholarly or research knowledge. This distinction should be visible where it affects interpretation.

Connected does not mean public.

Visibility may depend on identity, role, affiliation, relationship, consent, sensitivity, policy, legal requirements, security, and context. A relational model must not assume universal transparency.

Search and discovery

Search is a primary access mechanism to the relational model, but it is not limited to keyword matching. The intended discovery progression is:

Query → Entity → Context → Related Entities → New Discovery → Action

A user might search for a person and discover research, begin with a facility and find its responsible organization, or search for a course and find related opportunities. Relationship traversal is one of the central behaviors the MVP should test.

Community, knowledge, and place

CAMPUS may provide a community-facing environment for appropriately authorized contributions, discussions, activities, recommendations, and contextual knowledge. Attribution, source information, timestamps, verification, moderation, reporting, version history, permissions, and institutional endorsement are possible mechanisms; the governance model remains unresolved.

Physical space is part of the relational model rather than a separate subsystem. People can use facilities, facilities can be located in buildings, and buildings can belong to a campus. A map is one interface to these relationships, not the definition of the spatial architecture. A complete digital twin is not required for the initial implementation.

Integration boundary

CAMPUS exists alongside specialized institutional systems. It should not automatically become authoritative for information that belongs elsewhere. Depending on the system, it may represent references, metadata, relationship information, synchronized subsets, indexes, links, or derived information.

Integration Principle

CAMPUS should know enough to connect the university without needing to own everything the university knows.

Deployment directions

The conceptual architecture allows centralized, local-first, and hybrid deployment models. A centralized model connects users through a CAMPUS platform to institutional systems. A local-first model uses a campus-controlled node for users, systems, and data. A hybrid model combines local functionality with controlled synchronization or APIs to external services.

The hybrid direction is relevant to the current research because it may support local continuity while preserving controlled external connections. No deployment model is established yet.

Experience and future dimensions

Potential experiences include search, profiles, relationship navigation, community participation, place discovery, pathways, and knowledge discovery. These are experience categories rather than a commitment to implement every interface.

The relational core may support future dimensions such as Digital Space and Community Knowledge, Curriculum Continuity and Learning Records, Pathways, Research Discovery, Spatial Campus or Digital Twin, GIS, analytics, and AI. These should remain extensions of the foundation rather than independent product silos, and their priority must depend on evidence.

Architectural invariants

The following principles should remain stable unless evidence shows they should change:

  • 01 Relationships are central and discoverable.
  • 02 Existing systems retain appropriate authority.
  • 03 Identity and permissions are foundational.
  • 04 Provenance and context matter.
  • 05 Physical and digital contexts can connect.
  • 06 The architecture remains extensible.
  • 07 MVP scope remains bounded.
  • 08 Evidence can change the architecture.

Conceptual boundary

This architecture does not establish a database technology, programming language, application framework, graph versus relational database, API specification, deployment infrastructure, cloud provider, local hardware, synchronization protocol, identity provider, search engine, GIS platform, AI model, frontend framework, or production topology.

Those decisions belong in the technical architecture and should be derived from the requirements and findings established elsewhere in the blueprint. This conceptual architecture is the design hypothesis against which those technical alternatives can be evaluated.

Architectural Summary

CAMPUS does not attempt to digitize every university function. It attempts to make the relationships among university functions, people, knowledge, places, resources, and opportunities more persistent, discoverable, contextual, and actionable.
