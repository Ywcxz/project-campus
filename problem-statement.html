Problem Statement

The problem CAMPUS is investigating

A university is not a single information environment. It is a network of people, academic programs, courses, research activities, organizations, facilities, services, communities, institutional processes, physical spaces, external partners, and accumulated knowledge.

These entities are connected through numerous relationships, yet information about them may exist across different systems, offices, documents, websites, databases, physical locations, communities, and informal channels. CAMPUS investigates whether this fragmentation makes important relationships unnecessarily difficult to discover, understand, navigate, maintain, or act upon.

Problem Status · Proposed

The central problem is a research problem, not an established claim about VSU or any other institution. Its existence, severity, and appropriate response require institutional discovery, user research, technical experimentation, and prototype validation.

Information fragmentation

University information is often distributed across systems designed around particular institutional functions. One system may represent student records, another learning activities, another research outputs, another administrative information, and another physical facilities.

This specialization is not inherently a problem. It can provide authority, security, performance, governance, and operational focus. The research problem emerges when users need to understand relationships that cross those boundaries: which people or organizations are connected to a topic, what facilities support an activity, who is responsible for a service, or what opportunities follow from an educational experience.

Relationship discovery

Information can be available without its broader relationships being visible. A course record may state when and where a course occurs, while a wider relationship graph could connect that course to its program, instructor, research, laboratory, student organization, event, and opportunity.

CAMPUS investigates whether users need a persistent way to move from one relevant entity to related entities without repeatedly restarting searches across disconnected information environments. The question is not whether existing systems can store relationships, but whether relationships that already exist across the university are discoverable and usable from the user's perspective.

Continuity over time

University relationships change. Students progress through programs, courses change, faculty move between roles, research projects begin and end, organizations change membership, events disappear from active channels, facilities change availability, and students become alumni.

CAMPUS investigates whether a persistent relational model could preserve useful continuity across education, people and communities, research, institutional knowledge, physical spaces, opportunities, services, and education-to-career transitions. Persistence is not automatically desirable: retention, privacy, security, legal, and governance constraints still apply.

What relationships should persist, for how long, for whom, under what authority, and with what controls?

From discovery to action

Finding information is not equivalent to being able to act on it. A user may discover a course, project, facility, organization, service, event, or opportunity and still need to determine whether it is relevant, who is responsible, whether access is permitted, what prerequisites exist, and what action should happen next.

Discovery → Context → Relationship → Decision → Interaction

The prototype should investigate whether presenting relationships and contextual information can reduce friction between discovering something and understanding what can be done with it.

Institutional knowledge and provenance

Relevant university knowledge may exist in institutional documents, research publications, project records, community discussions, faculty and staff expertise, student experiences, alumni networks, physical locations, informal institutional knowledge, and external partnerships.

These sources have different levels of authority, reliability, permanence, and sensitivity. Aggregating them without distinguishing provenance could create new problems. CAMPUS therefore treats the distinction between official information, community contributions, personal opinions, unverified information, and scholarly knowledge as part of the problem itself.

The physical-digital disconnect

University activities are simultaneously digital and physical. Students use digital resources in physical classes; researchers use laboratories while collaborating through digital systems; services operate from offices; and events occur in spaces but are discovered online.

CAMPUS investigates whether representing meaningful relationships between digital entities and physical places can improve discovery and interaction. This may support later spatial or digital-twin work, but it does not establish that a full digital twin is necessary.

System boundaries

A relational layer could duplicate information already held elsewhere. If it becomes another independent source of truth, it may increase duplication, synchronization complexity, security exposure, governance burden, conflicting information, and institutional cost.

The central systems-design question is therefore how a relational discovery layer can provide meaningful cross-system relationships without unnecessarily duplicating or replacing authoritative systems. APIs, federation, indexing, metadata references, controlled synchronization, event-driven integration, and local replicas are possible approaches, not established decisions.

Institutional context

Before CAMPUS can be evaluated as a university architecture, the existing institutional environment must be understood: current systems, authoritative sources, ownership, identity management, interfaces, data exchange rules, privacy and security requirements, policies, infrastructure, constraints, and the workarounds users employ when existing systems do not meet their needs.

Institutional discovery must therefore precede substantial claims about integration, deployment, or a specific VSU systems deficiency.

Who may experience the problem?

Students may struggle to discover relevant people, opportunities, services, organizations, research activities, facilities, and pathways across organizational or system boundaries. Faculty and researchers may find collaboration, expertise, facilities, and institutional knowledge difficult to discover across departments. Staff and administrators may need to connect services, policies, offices, resources, and responsibilities. Alumni, partners, and communities may encounter limited continuity with university knowledge and opportunities.

What CAMPUS does not claim

CAMPUS does not claim that existing systems are inadequate, that all information should be centralized or public, or that a relational layer will automatically solve university coordination problems. It does not claim institutional adoption, validated impact, or a need to replace specialized systems.

Preliminary Problem Formulation

University resources and relationships may be distributed across institutional, technical, physical, and community boundaries in ways that make discovery, context, continuity, and action more difficult than necessary. CAMPUS investigates whether a persistent, permission-aware, provenance-aware relational layer can reduce that difficulty without undermining authority, privacy, governance, or system boundaries.

Evidence Required

The next research work must establish whether these potential problems occur in meaningful form, for whom, under what conditions, with what consequences, and whether the proposed CAMPUS approach is better than simpler alternatives.
