MVP Scope

The smallest prototype that can test the architecture

The CAMPUS MVP is a bounded functional vertical slice designed to test the central hypothesis: can a relationship-oriented digital layer make relevant university people, knowledge, places, resources, services, and opportunities easier to discover, understand, and act upon than disconnected information environments alone?

The MVP is a research instrument as well as a software prototype. It should generate evidence about user value, relationship discovery, context, technical feasibility, interoperability, identity, permissions, provenance, institutional fit, and development effort. It is not a complete CAMPUS deployment.

MVP Design Principle

Small enough to build, large enough to demonstrate the architecture, focused enough to evaluate, and extensible enough to learn from. Prioritize depth of relationships over breadth of features.

What the MVP must prove

The prototype should investigate five propositions: relevant entities and relationships can be represented coherently; users can discover useful information through relationships; context and provenance help users understand what they find; discovery can lead to an appropriate next action; and the relational layer can coexist with existing systems without becoming the system of record for every domain.

Primary MVP users

Students are the primary discovery-oriented users, looking for people, organizations, courses, programs, facilities, services, activities, and opportunities. Faculty and staff provide perspectives involving expertise, courses, organizations, facilities, services, research, activities, and institutional information.

University administrators test information authority, organizational relationships, service discovery, governance, permissions, system boundaries, and institutional context. Researchers, alumni, external partners, and visitors may participate in later research or prototype stages.

Core user scenarios

Discover a person through a relationship. A student finds a person and navigates through their role, organization, courses, research, activities, publications, and related opportunities. This tests whether contextual relationships provide more value than a simple directory profile.

Discover a resource through a need. A user searches for a service, facility, organization, or resource and sees its location, responsible unit, related services, people, and activities. This tests movement from discovery to context and availability.

Explore an entity. A user begins with a course, organization, facility, research topic, event, or service and follows relevant relationships. This tests relationship-oriented exploration.

Discover an opportunity. A student reaches an opportunity through a program, research area, project, or other existing relationship. This tests whether the model enables useful discovery beyond isolated information pages.

Navigate a physical resource. A user moves from a service or facility to its responsible organization, building, campus, or location. This tests the connection between digital information and physical space.

Canonical MVP model

The initial prototype should use a deliberately limited vocabulary: Person, Organization, Program, Course, Knowledge Resource, Research Project, Facility, Place, Event or Activity, Service, and Opportunity. The selected scenarios determine the minimum useful entity set.

Candidate relationships include membership, teaching, participation, authorship, research involvement, program membership, location, service provision, event location, opportunity association, facility use, and knowledge relationships. The goal is an understandable model, not a universal ontology.

Context, provenance, and permissions

Relationships should demonstrate more than a hyperlink. Where practical, they should carry type, status, temporal validity, source, provenance, visibility, responsible authority, and contextual description.

The prototype should distinguish official, community, personal, unverified, and scholarly knowledge. It should indicate source, author, date, verification status, or responsible organization where relevant, and must not present experimental or community content as official institutional information.

Basic identity and role concepts should demonstrate that users have distinct identities, information can have different visibility, and contribution authority can vary by role. The MVP must not assume universal access.

Search, profiles, and place

Search → Entity → Context → Related Entity → New Context → Action

Search should support both direct discovery and relational discovery. Selected entity profiles should function as relationship hubs rather than static pages. The MVP should include a minimal spatial component so a facility can connect to a building and campus, with a map or location view where useful.

Integration and data strategy

The prototype should demonstrate integration principles without requiring full institutional integration. Possible approaches include mock data, controlled samples, public data, references to authoritative sources, simulated APIs, or one authorized real integration.

Simulated integration must be clearly distinguished from actual VSU integration. Data should progress from synthetic or sample data to public non-sensitive data, controlled institutional data, and only then authorized pilot data when permissions, governance, and security controls exist.

Explicit exclusions

  • 01 Full curriculum-continuity or learning-record system.
  • 02 Complete education-to-career pathway platform.
  • 03 Full research collaboration environment.
  • 04 Sophisticated digital twin or advanced GIS.
  • 05 Large-scale institutional analytics.
  • 06 Production AI.
  • 07 Production-grade offline or local-first deployment.
  • 08 Full integration with every institutional system.
  • 09 Replacement of existing university systems.

The MVP should not become the ERP, LMS, student information system, research repository, centralized public data store, unrestricted social network, or a demonstration of every CAMPUS dimension. It must not imply VSU adoption or optimize for feature count.

Validation tasks and criteria

Early validation can ask users to find a person associated with a course, organization, or topic; find a related facility or service; discover an unexpected useful relationship; determine whether information is official, community-generated, scholarly, or unverified; and identify the appropriate next action.

Measures may include discovery success, time, navigation steps, confidence, relevance, effort, provenance comprehension, and action completion. Evaluation should cover user value, technical feasibility, institutional feasibility, and responsible operation. User enthusiasm alone is not validation.

Decision Gate

Strong evidence may justify expansion. Mixed evidence should lead to revision. Weak evidence may require narrowing, research-only work, stopping, or redirecting the project.

MVP boundary

The CAMPUS MVP is a small functional digital-campus prototype that represents a limited set of people, organizations, knowledge, places, activities, services, opportunities, and related entities; lets users search and navigate their relationships; provides basic identity, permissions, provenance, and context; and demonstrates coexistence with external or institutional information sources.

Build enough CAMPUS to learn whether CAMPUS itself is worth building further.
