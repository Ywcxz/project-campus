Research & Validation Methodology

Building to generate evidence

This methodology defines how CAMPUS will be investigated, prototyped, tested, evaluated, revised, and potentially discontinued. It treats CAMPUS as an R&D hypothesis rather than a predetermined product.

The objective is to determine whether the underlying problem exists in meaningful form, which relationships users need, whether a relational layer creates measurable value, whether the architecture is feasible, whether it can coexist responsibly with existing systems, and what conditions are required for further development.

Methodological Principle

CAMPUS should be built to generate evidence, not evidence manufactured to justify CAMPUS. The prototype is both a software artifact and a research instrument.

Evidence-driven R&D

Problem → Evidence → Hypothesis → Prototype → Test → Evidence → Revision → Decision

The project must avoid both technology-first development and confirmation-driven research. Findings should be able to strengthen the architecture, narrow the scope, modify assumptions, replace mechanisms, postpone dimensions, identify existing solutions, or disprove parts of the hypothesis.

Evidence classification

Major findings should be classified as Established when supported by reliable documents, observation, system behavior, literature, experiments, user testing, or formal decisions; Proposed when they are current hypotheses, designs, assumptions, or directions; and Unresolved when they require research, technical investigation, stakeholder input, clarification, or testing.

Research streams

  • A Problem and user research.
  • B Relational model research.
  • C User experience research.
  • D Institutional systems research.
  • E Technical architecture research.
  • F Governance, privacy, and security research.
  • G Local-first and hybrid architecture research.
  • H Future-dimension research.

Research phases

Discovery maps users, problems, workflows, systems, institutional context, constraints, and relevant research. Concept validation tests entities, relationships, discovery patterns, provenance, and user value. Technical feasibility tests representation, search, traversal, authorization, provenance, integration, deployment, and local-first mechanisms.

Functional prototyping builds a small vertical slice. User validation tests representative tasks. Institutional validation evaluates coexistence, governance, data access, security, operations, and usefulness. Decision determines whether directions expand, continue as experiments, revise, narrow, remain research-only, or stop.

User and workflow research

Research should investigate what users are trying to accomplish and where relationships become difficult to discover or use. Potential participants include students, faculty, researchers, staff, technical personnel, leadership, alumni, and selected external stakeholders.

Interviews and observation may examine discovery, relationships, continuity, existing systems, friction, and movement from information to action. Activities should produce structured artifacts such as interview notes, anonymized observations, workflow maps, journey maps, task definitions, relationship maps, evidence records, and decisions.

Relational model validation

The model should be tested independently from the visual interface. The key question is whether explicitly representing a relationship provides information that would otherwise be difficult to discover.

Tests may cover direct discovery, relational discovery, contextual discovery, unexpected discovery, and action discovery. Each candidate relationship should be evaluated for user benefit, supported action, stability, source, authority, validity period, privacy, duplication, and actual use.

Comparative prototype testing

Where practical, users should complete equivalent tasks through an existing or conventional workflow and through the CAMPUS prototype. Measures may include task completion, time, navigation steps, errors, comprehension, confidence, relationship recognition, and ability to proceed to an action.

The objective is not to guarantee that CAMPUS performs better. A result showing no meaningful improvement is an important finding.

Provenance, privacy, and trust

Users should be able to distinguish official institutional, community-generated, personal, unverified, scholarly, and externally sourced information. Testing should examine whether they understand source, verification, authority, age, and responsible ownership.

The ability to represent a relationship does not automatically justify representing it.

Privacy research must consider minimization, purpose limitation, visibility, consent, role-based and relationship-level access, retention, correction, deletion or de-identification, auditability, and sensitive relationship inference. Governance research must also distinguish technical permission from institutional authority.

Institutional and technical research

Institutional systems research should create an inventory covering systems, functions, owners, operators, authority, users, data, interfaces, identity, integration, security, privacy, lifecycle, relevance, and evidence status.

Technical experiments should test relationship representation and traversal, search, provenance, authorization, external integration, temporal relationships, local-first operation, synchronization, and extensibility. Local-first work must compare value gained against complexity introduced rather than treating local operation as inherently superior.

Ethics and participant protection

User research should use appropriate safeguards, including voluntary participation, privacy protection, anonymization, secure research records, minimized personal data, and clear separation between research participation and institutional evaluation. Formal human-participant research may require ethics review or another formal process.

Research log and decision records

Significant findings should record the date, activity, question, participants or source, observation, evidence, interpretation, confidence, affected assumption, resulting decision, and follow-up. Decision records should capture the decision, context, options, evidence, chosen direction, reason, trade-offs, consequences, status, and revisit condition.

Validation gates

  • 01 Problem validation: is the underlying problem meaningful?
  • 02 Relational value: do explicit relationships add value?
  • 03 Technical feasibility: can the model work acceptably?
  • 04 Institutional compatibility: can it coexist responsibly?
  • 05 User value: does it improve meaningful tasks?
  • 06 Expansion: is there evidence to move beyond the foundation?

Each gate can result in proceeding, revising, narrowing, restricting work to research, or stopping a direction. An institutional pilot should additionally require a defined problem and users, bounded use case, prototype evidence, system boundaries, data authority, privacy, security, governance, stakeholder support, and success and failure criteria.

Research outputs

Expected outputs include stakeholder and problem maps, workflow and architecture maps, a validated or revised entity and relationship model, provenance and permissions models, technical experiments, benchmarks, decision records, integration prototypes, local-first experiments, threat models, prototype versions, usability findings, institutional compatibility assessments, limitations, unresolved questions, expansion criteria, and disconfirmation findings.

Research-to-Architecture Loop

Research question → evidence collection → finding → architecture, UX, or governance implication → prototype change → new test → decision → blueprint update.

Research success

Research success is not defined as proving that CAMPUS should be adopted. It is successful when it produces sufficiently reliable evidence to answer important questions, including whether the full architecture, a narrower capability, a different integration model, a research-only direction, or no continuation is justified.

Methodology Conclusion

CAMPUS should progress as an evidence-generating R&D program: understand the problem, map the institutional environment, identify valuable relationships, test the model, build a small vertical slice, evaluate users and technical assumptions, assess governance and compatibility, and decide what deserves further investment.
