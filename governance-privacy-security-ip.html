Governance, Privacy, Security & Intellectual Property

Making relationships discoverable without making them indiscriminately visible

Governance, privacy, security, provenance, identity, authorization, and intellectual property are foundational to CAMPUS because the project represents relationships among people, organizations, knowledge, education, research, places, services, opportunities, and institutional resources.

Connecting information can create value and risk. The ability to represent a relationship does not automatically justify creating, exposing, retaining, or inferring it.

Governing Principle

Governance rules should be represented in the architecture wherever they affect what entities and relationships may exist, who can create or modify them, who can see them, how they are verified, how long they remain valid, and how disputes are handled.

Information authority

CAMPUS should distinguish official institutional information, community-generated information, personal opinion, unverified information, research or scholarly information, and external information. These categories should be distinguishable in the data model and, where relevant, in the interface.

Provenance and source of truth

Important information should identify its source, creator, publisher or authority, creation and update times, verification status, verification actor, import method, external identifier, and version where appropriate.

CAMPUS must distinguish the authoritative source of truth from its own copy, reference, index, projection, or relationship representation. An academic system may remain authoritative for enrollment while CAMPUS provides contextual discovery around a student, course, organization, or opportunity.

Relationship authority and lifecycle

Each important relationship may need an asserting authority, evidence, validity period, verification state, visibility, and authorized editors. A relationship can move through proposed, created, verified, active, updated, expired or superseded, and archived or removed states.

The relationship is not merely a line between two objects. It is a governed information object with type, status, provenance, authority, visibility, permissions, timestamps, and audit history.

Identity and authorization

Authentication → Identity → Affiliation or Role → Authorization → Action

Authentication does not automatically determine what a person may do. Potential roles include visitor, student, faculty, researcher, staff, administrator, contributor, moderator, institutional verifier, and system administrator. Actual roles and permissions must be established through institutional discovery.

Authorization may apply at system, organization, entity, relationship, field, and action levels. The MVP needs only the minimum permission model required for safe experimentation, not the complete governance architecture of a university-wide production service.

Connected does not mean public.

The existence of a relationship does not imply public visibility. Authorized users may see different attributes, aggregate information, or no relationship at all. Visibility must be treated separately from existence.

Privacy by design

Privacy requires data minimization, purpose limitation, access and retention limits, accuracy and correction, transparency, and security. CAMPUS introduces relational privacy risk because individually harmless information may become sensitive when connected, such as a person with an organization, location, project, event, community, or opportunity.

The relevant question is not only whether information is public, but whether exposing the relationship creates additional privacy risk.

Inference and AI governance

The system should distinguish explicit relationships from relationships derived from data or inferred through analysis or AI. Derived and inferred relationships require additional governance and must not be presented automatically as factual.

A future AI interface must pass through CAMPUS authorization and context before accessing governed knowledge and relationships. AI must not bypass permissions or become an independent source of truth, and responses should preserve appropriate provenance.

Community and institutional governance

Community information may require contribution, editing, reporting, moderation, verification, correction, dispute, appeal, archival, and removal mechanisms. The exact model depends on whether CAMPUS is a research prototype, university service, community platform, or institutional information layer.

Official institutional information should have stronger authority controls, designated owners, controlled publishing, approval workflows, audit history, source attribution, and review or expiration dates. The aim is to distinguish institutional authority from community contribution, not to prevent community participation.

Correction, retention, and auditability

CAMPUS should support reporting incorrect, outdated, misleading, improperly attributed, unauthorized, or privacy-sensitive information. A report may proceed through review, verification, correction, restriction, removal, and a recorded decision. Institutional correction authority should remain with the appropriate institutional source.

Retention must distinguish current, historical, archived, deleted, and anonymized information according to legal requirements, policy, research needs, authority, user rights, and historical value. Governance-sensitive actions such as relationship changes, verification, permission changes, administrative access, removal, integration, and synchronization should be auditable.

Security and data classification

Security spans identity, authorization, application, data, infrastructure, integration, and operations. Threat modeling should address unauthorized access, account compromise, privilege escalation, malicious content, relationship manipulation, leakage, inference, compromised integrations, synchronization attacks, insider misuse, infrastructure compromise, and denial of service.

Testing can progress from secure configuration, basic authorization, dependency scanning, input validation, and vulnerability assessment in the MVP to formal review and penetration testing in a pilot and continuous vulnerability management and incident response in production.

Before institutional deployment, data classification should influence storage, access, sharing, indexing, synchronization, retention, and logging. Local-first nodes additionally require secure registration, encryption, synchronization authentication, revocation, recovery, conflict handling, and monitoring. Local-first is not inherently more secure; its properties must be demonstrated.

Intellectual property

Potential intellectual property includes source code, architecture, documentation, interface designs, data models, research outputs, datasets, visual materials, algorithms, experiments, project identity, and possible future inventions.

Ownership depends on creators, employment or contracts, funding, institutional policies, collaboration agreements, third-party components, licenses, and research arrangements. Because CAMPUS is independently initiated, records should preserve original work, creation dates, contributors, source materials, third-party components, institutional contributions, and collaborative modifications.

If institutional collaboration begins, appropriate agreements should address ownership, licensing, use rights, publication, confidentiality, data, derivative works, and commercialization. Open-source licensing may support transparency, reproducibility, collaboration, and portability, but license compatibility, security, support, and institutional requirements remain unresolved.

Third-party tools and research data

Open-source libraries, commercial software, cloud services, APIs, and AI tools should be assessed for licensing, data use, privacy, security, vendor dependency, exportability, cost, and availability. Sensitive institutional information should not be sent to third-party services without appropriate authorization and data-protection conditions.

Research data such as interview notes, usability results, prototype logs, synthetic data, anonymized data, and experimental datasets need defined purpose, access control, retention, anonymization, documentation, and secure storage distinct from production data.

Responsibility and separation of concerns

Governance responsibilities may be distributed among technical operators, identity authorities, data owners, relationship verifiers, moderators, security and privacy authorities, research governance, rights holders, and strategic decision-makers. These are conceptual responsibilities, not assigned VSU roles.

A technical administrator does not automatically become the institutional information authority, and a content moderator does not automatically become the data owner. Separating technical, informational, institutional, and governance authority reduces concentration of control.

Pilot and external-participant governance

Before a pilot, an explicit arrangement should define participating units, system owner, operator, data owners, authorized data, user groups, security and privacy responsibilities, incident and correction processes, termination, evaluation, and data disposition. The form may be an agreement, memorandum, charter, or another appropriate instrument.

Alumni, employers, researchers from other institutions, and other external participants introduce additional questions about identity, affiliation, permission, authority, ownership, privacy, and moderation. External participation should be introduced deliberately.

Principles of least authority and reversibility

Each actor, integration, API, service, AI system, and local node should receive only the authority required for its legitimate function. Governance-sensitive actions should be reversible or reviewable where possible, with stronger authorization for irreversible actions.

MVP governance boundary

The MVP should demonstrate basic identity, role-based permissions, provenance, source and authority distinctions, visibility, relationship ownership or creation authority, basic auditability, and correction capability where appropriate. It does not need to implement the full governance architecture required for university-wide production.

Every future dimension requires its own governance assessment. CCLR may involve educational records, Pathways career information, Research confidential work and intellectual property, Spatial facilities, Local-First distributed stewardship, Analytics inference, and AI disclosure or access-control risks.

Governance Failure Conditions

Broader deployment should not proceed if authority is unclear, permissions cannot be enforced, sensitive relationships are exposed, provenance cannot be established, institutional information cannot be distinguished, security or privacy risks are disproportionate, or responsibilities are undefined.

Governance decision framework

For each information or relationship type, ask what it is, why it is represented, who owns it, who may create, verify, modify, or see it, how provenance is established, how long it remains valid, how it is corrected or removed, what happens when it is disputed, what privacy and security risks it creates, and which system remains authoritative.

Governance Conclusion

CAMPUS should make relationships discoverable without making them indiscriminately visible, authoritative without pretending all information is authoritative, persistent without making all information permanent, and connected without removing boundaries that protect people and institutions.

Identity is not authority. Connection is not public visibility. Information is not verified truth. Technical capability is not institutional permission.
